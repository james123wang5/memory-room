3D recreation of Neos apartment from The Matrix, built 100% using Claude via MCP connected to Blender. It’s not a perfect 1:1 recreation due to creative liberties and limited reference material. Inspired by [hirokazu yokohara](https://jp.linkedin.com/in/hirokazuyokohara?trk=public_post-text) project, I tackled this workflow with one main difference: mine is 100% modeled by Claude from scratch, no Image\-to\-3D tools like Meshy or Tripo were used. It took roughly 5 days part\-time. Creating an environment like this traditionally would easily take 1 to 2 months. I used Claude Opus 5.5 alongside parallel agents with Sonnet and Haiku. Claude handled execution, modeling, UVs, camera, materials, and compositing, guided heavily by my drawn annotations and feedback. Working within a basic $20 plan was the main hurdle, requiring strict token management and waiting out 5\-hour resets. A higher\-tier subscription is definitely recommended for heavy workflows. Producing a scene like this requires solid 3D fundamentals and art direction; a simple prompt isnt enough. Textures were a mix of external maps and vector graphics generated directly by Claude for posters and monitors. Claude researched set hardware, gathered blueprints, and modeled assets in parallel. Layout was the trickiest step to direct, whereas UV unwrapping was the fastest. Lighting and compositing were handled accurately from my sketch overlays. Fun fact: I directed much of the project right from my phone\! The pipeline ran locally using Windows MCP and Blender MCP, rendered on an RTX 3070. Feel free to ask any questions below\! Your After Effects experience is more useful in 3D than you think. We run a media art studio in Seoul, and we teach motion designers who are moving from AE into Blender and Unreal Engine. A common question is "do I have to start over?" Mostly, no. Here's how we split it. What you bring with you: 1. Timing. Shaping a speed curve in the Graph Editor works the same way in Blender and Unreal. Keyframes and easing don't change. 2. Thinking in layers. If you've built comps out of separate layers, render passes will make sense right away: colour, light and shadow rendered separately, finished in AE. The last place you touch the shot stays the same. 3. Your eye for framing. It matters more once the camera is real. What you leave behind: 1. Faking depth by stacking flat layers. 💥 Creating Surface Details in Substance Designer is one of the hardest things to do. But I managed to set up a series of rules/steps to make it easier\! I used to struggle a lot when building realistic materials. Mostly because my surface details weren't convincing enough. It always felt like the right details were missing in every material I did. So I added more and more, and my work looked worse after that. However, after 5 years of working as a Material Artist, I started to gradually change how I work. And came up with these steps to make sure I get these details right: 1️⃣ I started using only one reference for my surface detail reference to avoid confusing myself with so many options. 2️⃣ I followed the rule of 3 even in my details: start big, go medium, then end in smaller shapes. 3️⃣ I explored every setup, making sure I got the result I needed before moving to the next part of my material. 4️⃣ I applied all details with Logic & Soft values. 5️⃣ I made sure my work looked good by pre\-rendering before going to base color creation. This helped me get better results, but only when I started to apply them consistently and one step at a time. 🦾 Because, as Alex Hormozi said: "The key to exponential growth is doing fewer things, but better and more consistently." Hope this was useful for learning more about Material Creation. And if it was....
